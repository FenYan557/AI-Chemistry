## A B S T R A C T

Organic additives play an important role in regulating <sup>fi</sup>lm growth and properties in Cu electrodeposition. 3‑Mercapto‑1‑propane sulfonate (MPS) and its dimer bis(3‑sulfopropyl) disulfide (SPS) are representative accelerators that promote Cu electrodeposition rate. In this study, the formal reduction potential of SPS to MPS wa determined by measuring the equilibrium constants of thiol–disulfide interchange reactions with the reference thiol/disul<sup>fi</sup>de pairs by <sup>1</sup>H nuclear magnetic resonance (<sup>1</sup>H NMR) spectroscopy. The calculated formal reduction potential of SPS to MPS was 0.153 V (vs. standard hydrogen electrode). Based on this calculation, the cell potentials of Cu(I)(thiolate) formation reaction with either SPS or MPS, which is a key reaction in accelerating mechanism, were also estimated by ultraviolet–visible absorption and <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectroscopies: they were found to be 0.202 V and 0.214 V for SPS and MPS, respectively. The positive cell potential indicates that the formation of Cu(I)(thiolate) complexes is thermodynamically favorable in the presence of additives.

## 1. Introduction

Cu has been widely used as metallization material in the semi conductor industry owing to its low electrical resistivity and high electromigration resistance. Cu metallization is usually performed by electrodeposition, which e<sup>fi</sup>ciently yields high quality <sup>fi</sup>lms by a relatively simple and inexpensive process. Electrolyte for Cu electrodeposition typically contains small quantities of organic additives, which are essential to regulate <sup>fi</sup>lm growth and properties. Additives are grouped into accelerators, suppressors, and levelers according to their roles in controlling the deposition rate. Among them, 3‑mercap to‑1‑propanesulfonate (MPS) and its dimer bis(3‑sulfopropyl) disulfide (SPS) are the most representative accelerators in Cu electrodeposition [1].

The accelerating e<sup>f</sup>ect of SPS or MPS has been explained by two mechanisms [2]. One describes competitive adsorption between accel erator and suppressor; the displacement of suppressor inhibition layer by SPS or MPS facilitates the approach of Cu ions to electrode surface, enhancing the deposition rate $[ 3 - 7 ]$ . The other mechanism focuses on direct catalytic reduction of $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ ion to $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ ion by forming Cu(I) (thiolate) complex [8–11]. The reduction of ${ \mathsf { C u } } ^ { 2 + } { \mathsf { t o } } { \mathsf { C u } } ^ { + }$ is known as the rate-determining step of Cu electrodeposition [12]. Vereecken et al. reported that the amount of $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ near electrode surface is a key factor determining the deposition rate in Cu electrodeposition [13,14].

Nevertheless, only trace amounts of $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ can exist in the electrolyte, as $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ is easily oxidized to $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ by dissolved oxygen. MPS or SPS in electrolyte can form stable Cu(I)(thiolate) complex, increasing the amount of $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ species near the electrode surface [14]. The corresponding reactions are described as follows.

$$
4 \mathrm{MPS} + 2 \mathrm{Cu} ^ {2 +} \leftrightarrow \mathrm{SPS} + 2 \mathrm{Cu(I)(thiolate)} + 4 \mathrm{H} ^ {+}\tag{1}
$$

$$
\mathrm{SPS} + 4 \mathrm{Cu} ^ {+} \leftrightarrow 2 \mathrm{Cu(I)(thiolate)} + 2 \mathrm{Cu} ^ {2 +}\tag{2}
$$

In spite of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of these reactions, few thermodynamic data have been reported. Eqs. (1) and (2) are both complexation reactions including the redox reactions between $\mathsf { C u } ^ { 2 + } / \mathsf { C u } ^ { + }$ and SPS/MPS. Though the standard reduction potential of $\mathsf { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ to $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ is known as 0.159 V (vs. standard hydrogen electrode (SHE)) [15], the lack of reduction potential of SPS makes it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to obtain the cell potentials of Eqs. (1) and (2). The reduction reaction of SPS is shown by Eq. (3).

$$
\mathrm{SPS} + 2 \mathrm{H} ^ {+} + 2 \mathrm{e} ^ {-} \leftrightarrow 2 \mathrm{MPS}\tag{3}
$$

Herein, the formal reduction potential of SPS $( \operatorname { E q } .$ . (3)) was obtained, and the cell potentials of Eqs. (1) and (2) were estimated by spectroscopy. The formal reduction potential of SPS was obtained by measuring the chemical equilibrium constants of thiol–disulfide interchange reaction between MPS/SPS pairs and reference thiol/disul<sup>fi</sup>de pairs, lipoic acid (Lip) and mercaptoethanol (ME). The calculation provides thermodynamic information of accelerating mechanism by either SPS or MPS in Cu electrodeposition.

## 2. Experimental

## 2.1. Electrochemical analysis

Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed with electrolyte composed of 0.25 M CuSO and 1.0 M H SO . SPS or MPS was also added to the electrolyte. The concentrations of SPS and MPS were 50 μM and 100 μM, respectively, at which the equivalent concentration of thiolate functional group is same: one SPS is reduced to two MPS (Eq. (3)). The electrochemical analysis was conducted in a three-electrode system comprising a Cu rotating disk electrode $( 0 . 2 \thinspace { \mathrm { c m } } ^ { 2 } )$ , Cu rod, and $\mathbf { A g } / \mathbf { A g C l } ,$ /saturated KCl electrode $( \mathsf { A g / A g C l } )$ as the working electrode, the counter electrode, and the reference electrode, respectively. Temperature was held at $3 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (303 K) by thermostat during the analysis. The potential applied on the working electrode was swept from 0.15 V $\mathrm { t o } - 0 . 8 5 \ : \mathrm { V }$ (vs. Ag/AgCl) at a scan rate of 10 mV/s using a potentiostat (PAR 273A, EG&G Princeton Applied Research Corporation). All the LSV analysis was conducted right after the solution preparation to minimize the extent of reactions, Eqs. (1) and (2).

Chemical equilibrium constants were obtained by measuring the equilibrium concentrations of chemical species by $\mathrm { ^ { 1 } H }$ NMR spectroscopy (Bruker Avance III 400 FT-NM, Bruker). Two test samples were prepared in 5 mm NMR tubes. One comprised 10 mM Lip and 20 mM MPS and the other had 20 mM ME and 10 mM SPS, dissolved in the deuterated phosphate bu<sup>f</sup>er solution. The NMR tubes containing samples were sealed completely with polytetra<sup>fl</sup>uoroethylene (PTFE) tape and then kept on shelves at room temperature for 0–45 h before acquiring NMR spectra. The concentrations of the species were determined by measuring the peak areas of the following NMR resonances, Lip: 3.69 ppm, $\operatorname { L i p } ^ { \mathrm { r e d } } \colon$ 3.58 ppm, ME<sup>ox</sup>: 3.86 ppm, ME: 3.69 ppm, MPS: 2.66 ppm and 2.02 ppm, and SPS: 2.86 ppm and 2.15 ppm [17,18].

$\mathbf { D } _ { 2 } \mathbf { O }$ solution containing 0.05 M SPS, 0.05 M CuCl, and 0.25 M D SO was prepared to measure chemical the equilibrium constants of Cu(I)(thiolate) formation reactions. The solution was kept at room temperature for 0–15 h in a 5–mm NMR tube sealed with PTFE tape before acquiring NMR spectra. The characteristic <sup>1</sup>H NMR resonances of Cu(I)(thiolate) appeared at 3.15 ppm and 2.32 ppm, whereas <sup>1</sup>H NMR peaks for SPS appeared at 3.03 ppm, 2.86 ppm, and 2.15 ppm [18].

All the experiments were carried out under nitrogen atmosphere (99.9% purity) at room temperature (298 K) to avoid any oxidation of components by oxygen.

## 3. Results and discussion

## 3.1. Electrochemical analysis of acceleration efect by SPS or MPS

The accelerating e<sup>f</sup>ect of SPS or MPS in Cu electrodeposition was observed in LSV, as shown in Fig. 1. The addition of either SPS or MPS into deposition electrolyte increases Cu ion reduction current, indicating that the electrodeposition process is accelerated by direct catalytic reduction of $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ to $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ via formation of Cu(I)(thiolate) complex. It is notable that MPS exhibits slightly stronger acceleration e<sup>f</sup>ect than SPS at the same equivalent thiolate concentration, because MPS directly forms Cu(I)(thiolate) complex with major ${ \mathrm { C u } } ^ { 2 + }$ , whereas SPS only combines with minor $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ , coming from the comproportio nation reaction between $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ and metallic Cu (Eq. (4)).

$$
\mathrm{Cu} + \mathrm{Cu} ^ {2 +} \leftrightarrow 2 \mathrm{Cu} ^ {+}\tag{4}
$$

## 3.2. Formal reduction potential of SPS

As a pair of thiol/disul<sup>fi</sup>de, SPS and MPS can react with another thiol/disulfide pair via substitution, which is called thiol–disulfide interchange reaction [19–22]. The reduction potential of disulfide compound in thiol–disulfide interchange is usually obtained by establishing the equilibrium between the target material and a reference thiol/disul<sup>fi</sup>de pair of known redox potential, rather than electroanalytical measurements because many redox systems of sulfur are electrochemically irreversible $[ 1 5 , 1 7 , 2 3 , 2 4 ]$ . The irreversible characteristic leads to the unsteady electrode potential which is inconsistent with the Nernst equation, hindering the accurate calculation of reduction potential. Therefore, in this study, the thiol–disulfide interchange reaction was introduced with Lip and ME as the reference materials. The thiol–disulfide interchange reactions between SPS/MPS and Lip/ME are as follows.

$$
2 \mathrm{MPS} + \mathrm{Lip} \leftrightarrow \mathrm{SPS} + \mathrm{Lip} ^ {\mathrm{red}}\tag{5}
$$

$$
\mathrm{SPS} + 2 \mathrm{ME} \leftrightarrow 2 \mathrm{MPS} + \mathrm{ME} ^ {\mathrm{ox}}\tag{6}
$$

where superscript ‘red’ and ‘ox’ indicate the reduced and oxidized forms, respectively. The formal reduction potentials of Lip $( \mathrm { E _ { L i p } } )$ and $\mathbf { M E } ^ { \mathbf { o x } } \left( \mathbf { E } _ { \mathbf { M E } } \right)$ are 0.125 V and 0.153 V, respectively, with respect to the SHE [17]. The equilibrium constants $\mathrm { ( K _ { e q } ) }$ of Eqs. (5) and (6) are de-<sup>fi</sup>ned as follows:

In the conventional CuSO –based Cu electrodeposition solution, major Cu species in the electrolyte is $\mathsf { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ , not $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ . At this situation, there are two possible mechanisms for the formation of Cu(I)(thiolate) in the presence of SPS. One is that SPS is reduced to MPS and then reacts with $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ <sup>+</sup>. The other mechanism proposes that SPS forms Cu(I) (thiolate) complex with $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ formed via Eq. (4). However, many studies have shown that Cu(I)(thiolate) complex is formed via the latter mechanism, rather than the former [8,14]. Our UV–vis spectroscopy study also showed the same result (Fig. 4(e)). When $\mathrm { C u S O _ { 4 } }$ solution was mixed with the equivalent concentration of SPS in the presence of Cu foil fragment (2 mm × 2 mm), a strong absorption was observed in the range between 300 nm and 450 nm, related to Cu(I)(thiolate). In contrast, the solution containing SPS and $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ without Cu foil did not show the complex formation, supporting that Cu(I)(thiolate) is dominantly formed via Eq. (2).

The cell potentials of the two pathways for the formation of Cu(I) (thiolate) were then obtained. As Eq. (1) is expressed by combining Eqs. (2) and $^ { ( 3 ) , }$ and the reduction of ${ \mathsf { C u } } ^ { 2 + } { \mathsf { t o } } { \mathsf { C u } } ^ { + }$ , the cell potential of Eq. (1) can be calculated if the cell potential of Eq. (2) is known. Therefore, the cell potential of Eq. (2) was obtained first. <sup>1</sup>H NMR and UV–vis spectroscopies were used to measure the steady-state concentrations of the reactants and products in $\operatorname { E q . }$ (2). <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum of the reaction mixture at the steady state is shown in Fig. 5; peaks (1) and (2) represent the characteristic peaks of Cu(I)(thiolate) and SPS, respectively. The steady-state concentrations of SPS and Cu(I)(thiolate) were measured as 45.5 mM and 9.1 mM, respectively. Since <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectroscopy cannot detect either ${ \mathrm { C u } } ^ { 2 + }$ or Cu<sup>+</sup>, UV–vis spectroscopy was introduced for the measurement of $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ concentration. With the molar attenuation coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of $\mathbf { C } \mathbf { u } ^ { 2 + } \ ( 1 3 . 2 \mathbf { m o l } ^ { - 1 } { \cdot } \mathbf { c m } ^ { - 1 } )$ and the absorbance of $\mathrm { C u ^ { 2 + } }$ at 800 nm at the steady state $( \mathrm { F i g . ~ } 4 ( \mathsf { d } ) \colon \mathsf { C u } ^ { + } \mathrm { ~ + ~ } \mathsf { S P S } )$ , the steadstate concentration of $\mathrm { C u ^ { 2 + } }$ was calculated as 14 mM. The concentration may consist of ${ \mathrm { C u } } ^ { 2 + }$ originating from CuCl because CuCl is easily oxidized in the air. As the CuCl used in this study contains 0.5% of ${ \mathrm { C u } } ^ { 2 + }$ , the concentration of $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ originating from $\mathrm { C u C l } _ { 2 }$ was estimated as $0 . 4$ mM in our experiment. This value was relatively small compared to the measured concentration. The steady-state concentration of $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ was considered as 0.47 mM on the assumption that CuCl dissolves up to its solubility limit $( 4 . 7 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 } \mathrm { M }$ at 20 °C) because the direct measurement of free $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ concentration was not possible. The major concern of this assumption is the existence of $\mathrm { C l } ^ { - }$ <sup>−</sup> in the system (Eq. (2)). The introduction of $\mathtt { C l } ^ { - }$ is inevitable and may cause some errors in the calculation if Eq. (2) were strongly a<sup>f</sup>ected by Cl<sup>−</sup>.

The $\mathrm { K } _ { \mathrm { e q } 2 }$ was then calculated as $7 . 0 1 \times 1 0 ^ { 6 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 }$ , corresponding to 0.202 V of the cell potential. Combining it with the measured formal potential of SPS and the standard reduction potential of $\mathrm { C u } ^ { 2 + }$ to $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ the cell potential of Eq. (1) was 0.214 V. Inversely, using the Nernst equation (Eq. (9)) with the potential value, the $\mathrm { K _ { e q 1 } }$ is 5 × 10<sup>3</sup> M, which corresponds well with the previously reported value $( 1 0 ^ { 3 . 3 } \mathbf { M } )$ [13,18]. This indicates indirectly that the assumption used in the estimation of free $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ concentration is acceptable. The positive cell potentials of Eqs. (1) and (2) indicate that they are thermodynamically favorable. This supports the spontaneous formation of Cu(I)(thiolate) complex and its catalytic function in accelerating Cu deposition process [8]. G.-Cardona et al. obtained $5 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } \mathrm { M } ^ { - 1 }$ for $\mathrm { K } _ { \mathrm { e q } 2 }$ [18], which is di<sup>f</sup>erent with our result. According to their result, the cell potential of Eq. (2) should be −0.067 V, implying that Eq. (2) is not spontaneous. This does not correspond to the generally known mechanism of Cu(I)(thiolate) formation in solution chemistry [8–11].

The thermodynamic information of half-cell reactions occurring in Cu electrodeposition electrolyte containing either SPS or MPS is listed in Table 2. We believe that the thermodynamic data obtained in this study will assist the mechanistic study of Cu electrodeposition.

## 4. Conclusions

The formal reduction potential of SPS was <sup>fi</sup>rst determined by using thiol–disulfide interchange reaction with ME and Lip. The calculated formal reduction potential of SPS under the standard condition was 0.153 V (vs. SHE) in aqueous solution. The cell potential of the formation of Cu(I)(thiolate) complexes, which is the key reaction to explain the accelerating mechanism, was also estimated based on the formal reduction potential of SPS. The cell potential of Cu(I)(thiolate) formation between $\mathrm { { C u } ^ { + } }$ and SPS was 0.202 V, and the reaction between ${ \mathrm { C u } } ^ { 2 + }$ and MPS was 0.214 V. We believe that the thermodynamic values obtained in this study would be helpful in understanding or developing industrial Cu electrodeposition involving SPS.
